About this chart

What the family tree on the front page is, where its material came from, and the places where it is known to misrepresent the history it draws.

What this is

A collection of material gathered from around the web and filed by variant, so that each claim's origin can be checked rather than taken on trust. There are 61 entries, one page each, and every page records what each source was actually read to say about that ruleset — its tile count, its hand size, how it handles flowers, how it scores, who pays whom — beside the citation the reading came from. The reason for filing it this way is that proper rulebooks can eventually be built from material that has been verified instead of assumed.

The tree records descent as the source chart claims it. That is a narrower statement than it appears: a node's position under its parent is a citation, not a conclusion. Where the claim behind a position is thin, the entry says so rather than quietly straightening it out, and two separate tags carry the weakness along with it. Confidence describes the sourcing behind a variant; evidence describes what happened when those sources were set against each other. A variant can be well documented by one witness or thinly documented by several, and keeping the two judgements apart is what stops one from being mistaken for the other.

Nothing here is presented as settled. Every page carries the sources it rests on, so a disagreement can be taken up with the source rather than with the tree, and correcting an entry is as welcome as adding one. The findings collect the places where sources contradict each other or fall silent; the references list every source used, with back-links to the variants each was cited for.

Where the material comes from

The tree was derived from a single compiled chart, kept in the repository beside the data generated from it. That chart leans on two frameworks. Tom Sloper's variant catalogue [1] supplies the measurable facts for most leaf nodes: the tile counts, hand sizes and flower handling that tell one named variant from another. The Mahjong Wiki's family tree and timeline [7] supplies the branch structure and the decade-by-decade chronology this tree's shape follows.

The second of those is much the weaker, and the chart is candid about it. The timeline the branch structure rests on carries no citation on any page of the wiki: the 1950s adoption of Clear Chapter, the 1970s three-faan system and the late-1980s move to additive scoring are asserted and never sourced. What the wiki does credit is Cantonese — one named book [27], one named ruleset [28], and three Cantonese fan-list traditions with almost no English-language presence [29]. Sourcing is therefore uneven by region rather than absent: the Hong Kong material is traceable, while most of the Japanese, Nanyang, Korean and mainland material is not. Weight the tree accordingly, and weight anything filed into it accordingly too.

How to read the tree

Colour marks the family a ruleset belongs to, and a link between two nodes means the source chart claims descent between them. Two of those links were drawn dashed in the chart, meaning no source establishes the descent at all: Nanyang variants and Korean traditional. Both are filed where the chart filed them, and tagged so that the guess stays visible instead of hardening into a fact.

The sourcing behind the 61 entries breaks down as follows. A placeholder tag is a statement about the sources rather than about the game: it means nobody has established where a ruleset came from, not that the ruleset itself is doubtful.

Known limits of this tree

Any tree layout of this material distorts it, and the source chart is explicit about where. Each warning below is given in the chart's own words first, then read against the entries it affects.

Influence ran both ways between Hong Kong and Taiwan

Guangdong was influenced first by Hong Kong, later by Taiwanese; the arrow should run both ways.

The chart gives Guangdong style one parent, Hong Kong old style, because a single parent is the only shape a tree can hold. The account behind it is messier: Guangdong play was shaped first by Hong Kong, and then by Taiwanese play once the 16-tile game had established itself across the strait.

The entry keeps the single arrow and records the rest as a caveat, so the parentage drawn here is a sourced claim rather than the whole history. The late-1980s move to additive scoring belongs to that second phase, and is asserted on the wiki's timeline without a citation anywhere — which is why the node can be well documented for its rules and still carry a warning about its chronology.

Entries affected: Guangdong style, Taiwanese style · Sources: [42] [8] [28]

The mainland branch changed its frame mid-history

Mainland regionals began on a Chinese Classical frame and later adopted HKOS's simplified scoring.

The mainland regional games are filed under Shanghai / Hong Kong new style, which is where they began: a Chinese Classical frame, with the tile set and hand structure that implies. What they score with arrived later and from elsewhere, after Hong Kong old style had dropped fu in the 1950s and simplified scoring to something a table could total in its head.

A node has one position, so the tree records the frame and loses the borrowing. Read the eleven mainland entries as games with two inheritances: an older skeleton, and a newer scoring layer that came back up the branch from Hong Kong.

Entries affected: Hong Kong old style · Sources: [8] [7]

Some scoring travelled backwards, from West to East

All Pairs and All Green flowed backwards from Western Mah Jong into most other modern rulesets, meaning the us branch fed the cn branch, which no tree layout shows.

A family tree can only draw divergence. This one shows the Western branch leaving the Chinese line in 1920 with Babcock's rules and never rejoining it, yet the wiki's own Western Mah Jong scoring page credits British play with All Green, Seven Pairs and the Jewel Dragons — hands that then entered rulesets across the Chinese branch.

No book is named for that attribution, so it is recorded here as the wiki's claim rather than as an established fact. The direction still matters for anyone using the tree to date a hand: a pattern found in a Chinese ruleset is not automatically older than the Western one beside it.

Entries affected: Babcock rules, Western / British Empire style · Sources: [32]

Eleven nodes stand in for more than a hundred games

The eleven mainland nodes stand in for a hundred-plus real variants, most undocumented in English.

The mainland branch is the most compressed part of the tree. Its eleven entries are the ones with an English-language description attached; the real distribution of play is regional to a degree no tree of this size could represent, and most of those games have never been written up in English at all.

The effect is that the tree's shape is partly a map of English-language documentation rather than of play. A thin branch is not evidence that little is played there: Sichuan alone carries three entries here and far more variation than three in practice.

Entries affected: Sichuan / Hunan / Tibetan, Sichuan bloody rules, Sichuan 72-tile game · Sources: [1] [7]

Three entries rest on one person's report

Several entries (Shenzhen, Tui Dao Hu, Fujian) rest on a single informant report.

Shenzhen style, Tui Dao Hu and Fujian style each come from a single informant, material of that kind having largely been argued out in the rec.games.mahjong archive before reaching the chart through Sloper's variant catalogue. Nothing independent confirms any of the three. The compendium tags a fourth the chart's own line does not name: the Sichuan 72-tile game, reported by one person.

They are kept rather than dropped, and tagged single-informant so the weakness travels with the entry instead of being lost in the tree around it. One report is a lead worth filing; it is not two sources agreeing, and nothing here quietly promotes it to one.

Entries affected: Shenzhen style, Tui Dao Hu, Fujian style, Sichuan 72-tile game · Sources: [1] [35] [24]

The Korean 104-tile game has no established parent

No source establishes how Korean 104-tile mahjong descends from anything; the link is a placeholder.

Korean traditional play uses 104 tiles and drops the bamboo suit entirely, which puts it structurally further from its neighbours than any other entry on the tree. The chart hangs it under Chinese classical and draws the link dashed: no source examined establishes that descent, or proposes another.

The entry is tagged placeholder for exactly that reason, and it is one of only two dashed links in the chart — the Nanyang group is the other. The honest reading is that its position is a filing decision, not a finding.

Entries affected: Korean traditional · Sources: [1] [7]

Filipino style sits on a branch its hand size argues against

Filipino style sits under Nanyang despite a 16-tile hand resembling Taiwanese. The distinguishing test is that Filipino play treats winds and dragons as flowers.

Filipino style is played with a 16-tile hand, which is the structural signature of the Taiwanese fork, and yet the chart files it under the Nanyang group. What keeps it there is its treatment of honours: Filipino play handles winds and dragons as flowers, which Taiwanese play does not.

That leaves the entry two levels of doubt deep, because the Nanyang link above it is itself dashed and tagged disputed. A shared hand size is evidence of structural kinship, not proof of descent, and this is the clearest case in the tree of those two things coming apart.

Entries affected: Filipino style, Nanyang variants · Sources: [1] [7]
